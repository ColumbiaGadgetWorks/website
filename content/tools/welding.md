Welding is how flat stock becomes a frame, a cart, a bracket, a gate, or a sculpture. The shop runs three processes, and which one you want depends on the job:

- **MIG** is the fastest to learn and the right choice for most mild-steel fabrication. If you have never welded before, you will start here.
- **TIG** is slower, far more controllable, and what you need for thin material, aluminum, stainless, and anything where the bead is going to be visible.
- **Stick** handles dirty, rusty, or outdoor work that the other two would fight you on.

Around the welders sit the tools that make up most of the actual work: a chop saw and bandsaw for cutting stock to length, angle grinders for prep and cleanup, a belt sander, clamps, and a steel welding table.

**What people make:** shop carts and fixtures, trailer and vehicle repairs, furniture frames, garden and yard hardware, art, and battlebot armor.

## Training

Welding requires training and a supervised session on scrap before you strike an arc alone. Training covers machine setup and settings, shielding gas, the safety gear, ventilation, and fire watch. Expect an hour for the basics and a fair amount of practice after that, because welding is a skill you build rather than a procedure you follow. Settings cheat sheets for common material thicknesses are on the [member wiki](https://wiki.comogadget.casa/).

## Safety

Welding makes ultraviolet light, sparks, fumes, and very hot metal, in that order of sneakiness.

- Helmet down, and warn anyone nearby before you strike. Arc flash is a sunburn on your eyes and you will not feel it until hours later.
- Leathers, gloves, closed-toe leather boots, and no synthetic clothing. Polyester melts.
- Ventilation on. Never weld galvanized, plated, or painted stock without stripping it first.
- Fire watch after you finish. Nothing flammable within the work area, and check the area again before you leave.
